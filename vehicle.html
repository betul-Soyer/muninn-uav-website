    The aircraft

    GöğeBakan — a tail-sitter for SUAS 2026

    GöğeBakan ("the one who gazes at the sky") is a next-generation High-Speed VTOL (HSVTOL) tail-sitter: it takes off nose-up like a rotorcraft, pitches over, and cruises wing-borne like an airplane — with a single unified propulsion system doing both jobs. This page is its living design documentation.

    Overview

    Systems engineering, not parts assembly

    Tail-sitters were patented by Nikola Tesla back in 1928: on paper they answered every problem in aviation, but the technology of the day and the pilot workload they demanded kept them out of operational service. Our question was whether today's technology finally makes the concept work — and GöğeBakan is our answer, an original design whose architecture we have patented.

    Unlike conventional hybrid VTOL platforms that bolt together multiple propulsion systems and mechanically complex transition mechanisms, GöğeBakan adopts a unified propulsion architecture: the same four motors power vertical flight, the pitch-over transition and cruise. That single decision reduces structural weight, removes whole classes of mechanical failure, cuts maintenance — and makes the aircraft roughly 30% more efficient than comparable hybrid VTOLs while preserving the aerodynamic efficiency of a fixed wing and the operational flexibility of vertical takeoff and landing.

    The architecture integrates the PX4 flight control system, an AI-enabled companion computer, onboard computer vision, autonomous mission management, a robust three-link communication setup and custom electronic subsystems into one modular, scalable framework. Low-level stabilization is deliberately separated from high-level perception and decision-making — for computational efficiency, fault tolerance and mission reliability.

    Configuration

    Airframe & flight regimes

    GöğeBakan is a carbon-fibre flying-wing tail-sitter. On the ground it stands nose-up on its wingtip fins. Its four rotors lift it vertically like a quadrotor; a pitch-over transition tips the nose forward until the wing takes the load, and the same four motors become cruise propulsion. Recovery is the mirror image — a Pugachev's Cobra manoeuvre bleeds off airspeed and stands the aircraft back on its tail for a vertical landing.

    Because there is no separate lift system and no tilt mechanism, the structure stays light and the failure-mode list stays short — the reason the architecture earned a patent. Elevons provide control authority in both regimes, and the central pod concentrates the payload: nadir camera, AI companion computer and the battery — placed first, before anything else could claim the space.

    Why a tail-sitter and not a quadplane or multirotor? See design decision #1.

    Mission profile

    Dynamic flight profiling

    Phase 1 · Cruise out

    Fixed-wing to the search area

    The 3.2 km waypoint route is flown wing-borne to maximize energy efficiency — hover minutes are spent only where they buy mission value.

    Phase 2 · Search & map

    Multicopter-mode grid

    Over the search zones GöğeBakan transitions to multicopter mode and flies a lawnmower pattern. The stabilized flight dynamics sharpen the camera data — better detections, and clean input for SLAM to build the orthomosaic map.

    Phase 3 · Return & land

    Fixed-wing home, vertical landing

    Transits between search zones and the return leg go back to fixed-wing mode to save time. At the launch point the aircraft transitions once more and lands vertically, autonomously.

    Avionics

    Avionics & datalinks

    Flight control

    PX4 handles attitude stabilization, flight modes and the autonomous transitions between hover and cruise — the regime changes that define a tail-sitter.

    Companion compute

    The AI companion computer runs the ROS 2 stack: YOLOv8s inference, SLAM, mission management and visual servoing — high-level decisions kept cleanly apart from low-level control.

    Three radios, three jobs

    2.4 GHz for the safety pilot, 915 MHz MAVLink telemetry for the GCS, 5 GHz IP for imagery and detections. Losing any one link never takes down the other two.

    Software

    Autonomy: detection, mapping, alignment

    Detection — a dataset we had to build

    At the mission's 45 m search altitude, standard pre-trained models (COCO-class) simply failed to find people and tents reliably. So the team built a custom aerial dataset and trained a YOLOv8s model on it. Component-level testing confirmed high-accuracy detection at the target altitude — the model earned its place on the aircraft the same way every other part does: by test.

    Mapping — SLAM orthomosaic

    During the multicopter-mode search, SLAM algorithms run on the collected imagery to produce a high-resolution orthomosaic map — stabilized grid flight making both the detections and the map sharper.

    Alignment — visual servoing

    Once a target is confirmed, visual servoing takes over: the control loop continuously analyzes camera frames and adjusts the aircraft's position in real time until it holds the desired spatial relationship with the target. Simulation testing verified the aircraft aligning itself over targets with this technique before any hardware was risked.

    Mission management — QGroundControl

    Mission planning, monitoring and failsafe configuration run on QGroundControl, the industry-standard ground station for PX4. We deliberately did not write our own: QGC is PX4-native, proven by thousands of operators and already familiar to every safety pilot. Engineering time belongs to the parts of the mission nobody has solved for us. Detections arrive separately over the IP link and are reviewed alongside it.

    How all of this was proven — from the Gazebo digital twin to the 45-minute full-mission run — lives on the testing page.

    Safety

    Safety systems & failsafes

    An autonomous aircraft earns trust by what it does when things go wrong.

    • Geofence: a hard airspace boundary in PX4 — crossing it triggers an immediate, automatic response, no human required.
    • RC link loss: automatic return-to-launch profile ending in a vertical landing at the designated point.
    • GCS link loss: the mission continues briefly, then degrades to return-to-launch if telemetry does not recover.
    • Battery failsafe: staged responses at two thresholds — first warn and re-plan, then land.
    • Independent kill: the safety pilot's 2.4 GHz link can cut the mission at any moment, independent of the companion computer.
    • Checklists, always: no flight without the preflight checklist and a written test card — including the boring flights. Especially the boring flights.
    • Crewed roles: every flight has a named pilot in command, GCS operator and spotter. Nobody multitasks safety.

    Design decisions

    The trade studies behind GöğeBakan

    Every major choice, with the options we rejected and why. This log grows as the campaign progresses.

    Decision 01 · Platform

    Tail-sitter over multirotor or quadplane

    Options: multirotor · conventional fixed-wing · quadplane hybrid · tail-sitter VTOL.

    Choice & rationale: tail-sitter. The mission demands both precise positioning (search, target alignment, VTOL) and fast, efficient cruise (3.2 km legs, transit between zones). A tail-sitter merges the multirotor's precision with the fixed wing's speed and aerodynamic efficiency in a single body — answering every mission constraint at once.

    Decision 02 · Propulsion

    One unified propulsion system

    Options: separate lift + cruise motor sets (quadplane-style) · tilt mechanisms · unified motor set.

    Choice & rationale: unified. Separate lift motors are dead weight in cruise; tilt mechanisms add mass and mechanical failure points. One set of four motors serving hover, transition and cruise means less structural weight, fewer things that can break, less maintenance and about 30% better efficiency than comparable hybrids — reliability by subtraction. The architecture is patented.

    Decision 03 · Mission profile

    Dynamic flight profiling by mission phase

    Options: fly everything in one regime · switch regimes dynamically per mission phase.

    Choice & rationale: dynamic profiling. Cruise legs in fixed-wing mode for energy efficiency; search in multicopter mode because stabilized flight sharpens camera data for detection and SLAM; returns in fixed-wing to beat the 45-minute clock; vertical landing for safety.

    Decision 04 · Perception

    Custom dataset + YOLOv8s over pre-trained models

    Options: COCO-class pre-trained detectors · fine-tuned model on a custom aerial dataset.

    Choice & rationale: custom. Component testing showed pre-trained models failing on people and tents at 45 m — the objects are simply too small and the viewpoint too unusual. We built our own dataset, trained YOLOv8s on it, and verified high accuracy at the exact operating altitude.

    Decision 05 · Compute architecture

    PX4 + ROS 2 companion, strictly separated

    Options: one computer doing everything · flight controller + AI companion with a clean interface.

    Choice & rationale: separation. Low-level stabilization must never compete with a neural network for compute. PX4 owns attitude and transitions; the ROS 2 companion owns perception and decisions. The split improves computational efficiency and fault tolerance — a perception crash cannot take down the aircraft.

    Decision 06 · Validation & tooling

    Digital-twin-first, QGroundControl over a home-made console

    Options: test on hardware early · validate everything in simulation first; build our own ground station · fly the industry standard.

    Choice & rationale: a Gazebo digital twin of GöğeBakan (in a Baylands world customized with person and tent models) validates every algorithm before hardware is risked. On the ground we fly QGroundControl rather than a console of our own: it is PX4-native, battle-tested by thousands of operators, and every hour not spent re-implementing a mission planner is an hour spent on the autonomy that is actually ours.

    Dated engineering context for each decision lives in the build log, our historical record.
